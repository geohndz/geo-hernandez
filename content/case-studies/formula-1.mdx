## Research

<StudyGroup>
Hardcore Formula 1 fans already know how to watch a race. What they do not know is how a two-hour Grand Prix feels in a headset: neck, battery, attention, and the second the car leaves the middle of the view.

I wrote the brief before I drew the layout. If you cannot last the race, find the board, and still watch the car, the interface has already failed.

<F1Research />
</StudyGroup>

## Solution

<StudyGroup>
### Core Concept

Recreating a television inside VR would have been the obvious move. It would also have ignored the platform.

I treated the experience the way a racing game treats a HUD. The broadcast stays in charge. Everything else is spatial: reachable, ranked, and allowed to recede.

**The platform augments the stream. It does not replace it.**

<F1Solution />
</StudyGroup>

## Design Evolution

<StudyGroup>
### Constraints

Quest-class headsets were the honest test. Largest consumer install base, limited battery, and a field of view that punishes anything you have to hunt for. Those were not limitations to work around later. They were the design strategy.

<F1Constraints />
</StudyGroup>

<StudyGroup>
### Iterations

I started on paper: who the layout was for, what a fan reaches for during a stint, and how a window should behave once you can put it anywhere. That sketch is the system in miniature. A fixed center, modular sides, and a rule that content can move without the layout moving.

<F1Exploration />
</StudyGroup>

<StudyGroup>
From paper I took three visual directions to screen. Familiar F1 color and type helped people recognize the sport. Unchecked, they also fought the stream for attention. Some of the work wanted to be a livery more than an interface.

<F1VisualDirections />

<F1Direction />
</StudyGroup>

<StudyGroup>
The window itself went through the same pass. Early chrome performed identity. The version that shipped is a quieter shell the content can live in: one anatomy, reused across every module.

<Figure src="/media/formula-1/process/window-chrome.png" alt="Four leaderboard chrome iterations, from a sharp navy frame to a rounded window with a top bar and inset content" caption="The leaderboard, four chrome passes in. Identity stayed. The frame got out of the way." />

The hybrid that shipped kept the identity, borrowed the logic of a racing HUD, and followed the rules of spatial computing: one anchor, quieter chrome, supporting information that knows it is supporting.
</StudyGroup>

## Spatial System

<StudyGroup>
<F1SpatialDecision />

A layout that can take a new module without a new frame needs a vocabulary first. Zones decide where things may land. Windows are the shells. Components are the content. Those three stay independent so the interface can change without the system changing underneath it.

<F1SpatialModel />
</StudyGroup>

## Final Design

<StudyGroup>
Each module is a job a fan already does during a stint: watch the race, check the board, switch an onboard, read a driver, listen to radio. Support sits further out on purpose. Race-control notifications and a world-anchored track map can interrupt you without rearranging the layout you already learned.

<Figure src="/media/formula-1/process/shapesxr.jpg" alt="ShapesXR scene of the F1 layout around a seated viewer, with the leaderboard and radio on the left, the cockpit stream in the center, and driver information on the right" caption="The layout in ShapesXR, built around a seated viewer. Left and right stay narrow so the stream can keep the middle." />
